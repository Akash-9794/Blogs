Reliable AI agents start with reliable, governed data.

Many businesses assume that building AI agents is primarily a technology challenge. In reality, the bigger challenge often lies in the data those agents depend on.

Adobe's 2026 AI and Digital Trends report found that 75% of organizations identify data integration and quality as their biggest challenge when implementing agentic AI, ahead of talent gaps (71%) and unclear ROI (68%).

Before investing in AI agents, businesses need to ask a more fundamental question: Is their data actually ready?

This article gives you a practical way to assess your data before investing time and money in AI agents.

What is an AI agent?

An AI agent is a system designed to achieve a goal. It can break that goal into steps, retrieve information from connected tools and systems, and carry out tasks across multiple stages. Depending on its design, it can also check results and involve a human when approval is needed.

A chatbot answers a question. An agent does something about it.

That difference is exactly why data matters so much more when businesses move from conversational AI to systems that can take action.

Why is data the real bottleneck for AI agents?

Because an agent acts on the information it can access. If that information is incorrect, outdated or scattered across systems, the resulting action can be incorrect too.

The risk is also different from a traditional chatbot. A chatbot using poor data may give someone an incorrect answer. An agent using poor data could send the wrong email, approve an incorrect request or update the wrong business record before anyone notices.

From fragmented data to trusted AI actions

What happens when an AI agent encounters messy data?

Consider an illustrative example of a travel company building an AI agent to calculate customer refunds.

The refund policy exists in three places:

  • A PDF document containing the 2022 policy.
  • A newer policy shared through email.
  • A spreadsheet maintained by the finance team.

Unfortunately, the three sources do not agree.

Resolve conflicting records before an agent acts

The agent is not necessarily malfunctioning. It retrieves information from the sources available to it and may select an outdated policy. As a result, a customer receives an incorrect refund estimate, and an employee has to investigate and correct the issue.

The solution is not simply to use a more powerful AI model.

The business needs to establish which source is authoritative, assign ownership, record when the policy was last updated, and make the agent identify the source behind its answer.

Key takeaway: AI reliability is not just a model problem. It is also a data governance problem.

How do you check if your data is ready? Five questions

Before building an AI agent, assess the specific workflow you want it to handle. Ask these five questions honestly. A partial yes should be treated as an area that needs improvement.

  1. Do you know where the answer lives?
    Can you identify the authoritative source for the information required by the agent? If the answer is spread across emails, spreadsheets, PDFs and multiple business applications, the agent may struggle to identify which information is correct.
  2. Is your data current, and does someone own it?
    Every important dataset or document should have a last-updated date, a clearly identified owner, and a process for reviewing and correcting outdated information.
  3. Do your systems agree on the basics?
    Different departments may define the same business terms differently. Sales, finance and customer support may each calculate revenue or define an active customer using different rules.
  4. Can you control what the agent is allowed to access?
    AI agents should only access information they are authorised to use. Review user and role-based permissions, sensitive information, connected-application access and approval requirements.
  5. Can you identify when the agent gets something wrong?
    Trace answers to original sources, review action logs, monitor unexpected results, escalate sensitive decisions to a human, and make corrections possible.

A simple data-readiness rule of thumb

This is a practical assessment framework, not a formal industry benchmark.

Five yesesYou have a reasonable foundation for a controlled pilot.
Three or four yesesStart with a narrow use case while addressing the gaps.
Two or fewerPrioritise data quality, ownership and governance before deployment.

The goal is not to make every dataset perfect. It is to make the data used by your first AI agent reliable enough for its intended task.

Where should you start?

Start with one workflow and one dataset.

Choose a repetitive business activity, such as answering internal policy questions, preparing weekly reports or retrieving customer information.

  1. Identify the information required for that workflow.
  2. Select and document the authoritative sources.
  3. Clean up inconsistent and outdated records.
  4. Assign ownership and establish access permissions.
  5. Test the agent using real-world scenarios.
  6. Introduce human approval for actions that carry significant risk.

Once the first workflow works reliably, you can expand towards more complex use cases.

Why does a semantic layer matter?

If your AI agent needs to answer questions involving business numbers, such as revenue, customer retention or churn, data quality alone may not be enough. You also need consistent business definitions.

This is where a semantic layer becomes useful.

A semantic layer is a governed layer that defines business metrics and translates terms such as revenue, profit and churn into consistent calculations. It helps different tools, dashboards and AI agents work with the same business definitions.

One shared meaning for every business tool

There is also emerging benchmark evidence supporting this approach. In its 2026 semantic-layer benchmark, dbt Labs compared text-to-SQL with semantic-layer-based querying across 11 business questions. The reported results were:

  • Claude Sonnet 4.6: 90.0% with text-to-SQL versus 98.2% with the semantic layer.
  • GPT-5.3-Codex: 84.1% with text-to-SQL versus 100% with the semantic layer.

These are results from a limited benchmark, not a guarantee of accuracy in every enterprise environment. The results nevertheless illustrate how consistent business definitions and better data modelling can improve AI-assisted analytics.

The broader lesson is that AI agents need both access to data and a reliable understanding of what that data means.

A practical perspective: Start small, build trust

The temptation is to automate several business processes as soon as an AI agent becomes available. But starting with too many workflows can make it difficult to identify data problems, measure results and maintain control.

A better approach is to select one valuable workflow, prepare its data, test the agent and measure its performance. Once the results are reliable, expand gradually.

Build trust in your data before expecting AI agents to act on it.

Frequently asked questions

What does AI-ready data mean?

AI-ready data is accurate, current, consistently defined, documented and access-controlled. It gives AI systems reliable information to work with instead of forcing them to guess between conflicting sources.

Do I need a data warehouse before building an AI agent?

Not always. A narrow pilot can work with a small collection of curated documents or connected data sources. However, agents working across multiple business systems often need better integration, consistent definitions and appropriate governance.

Is data readiness for AI agents the same as readiness for dashboards?

They overlap, but AI agents introduce additional considerations. A dashboard presents information for someone to interpret. An agent may use that information to perform an action. Therefore, permissions, audit trails, source traceability and human approval become especially important.

Who should own data quality?

Each important data source should have a clearly identified owner, ideally within the team responsible for maintaining or using that information. Shared accountability is useful, but leaving ownership undefined creates problems.
